Washington University

Washington University in St. Louis — a California native, he came to St. Louis in the early 1980s for WashU and earned degrees in biology and sociology, graduating 1987. Full scholarship on tuition; after the first year he paid for housing himself. He was headed toward neuroscience — which is where the company name Axon comes from, and worth saying out loud because it makes the name earned rather than decorative.

Palo Alto

Before St. Louis: Palo Alto schools — Palo Alto High and the district — while Silicon Valley was starting. Bike rides on Page Mill, IBM and Hewlett-Packard in the air, watching the industry form. He still keeps up with friends from that era who went huge in the dot-com years. He prefers where he lives now — small-town pace over a valley that got too fast.

Doorways

First job out of college, about 1989: Doorways in St. Louis — housing for people living with AIDS. About fifteen apartments at the start. Discrimination was intense. Landlords who would rent were scarce and charged a premium; the organization managed the clients and the rent. He saw a better way: use 501(c)(3) tax credits to buy a building instead of enriching those landlords. They acquired a rough multi-story apartment, rehabbed it, and housed people there. He is proud of that one. Doorways later grew into one of the country’s large housing programs for people living with AIDS. He was on the ground floor, with a business mind. The organization is still operating: doorwayshousing.org.

Then he went to the Missouri Department of Health and began HIV pre- and post-test counseling.

Public health

Metro AIDS in St. Louis was, by his account, the only remaining anonymous HIV testing site in Missouri besides Kansas City. A handful of counselors. Insurers dropped people who tested positive, so the site was inundated. He did thousands of pre-test sessions and hundreds of positive post-test interviews — work he was very good at, and would still do. He was a trainer of trainers for the CDC, including physicians, and wrote papers for the health department on anonymous testing. About two years there.

The department stopped letting counselors take patient calls. People waited outside anyway, because they needed risk-assessment help. That is the problem he could not leave sitting: a health department that would not take the call.

From that foundation he left to build Specialized Testing Services and the first national STAT patient-service-center network. He advocated for virtual medicine. In 1994 he invested in a legal fight that lasted a year and a half. Ask him for the case name if you need it; this page will not invent one.

What the companies taught

The rest of the education is operating: call centers, labs, restaurants, historic property, 1031 exchanges, patents, and now voice AI. If you need a conventional transcript line beyond WashU 1987, ask him.
