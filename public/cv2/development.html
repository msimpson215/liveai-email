Historic Maplewood — from Maple Hood to a district

Marty used to call it Maple Hood, not Maplewood. When he started, the Sutton Loop corridor was rough — by his account it carried the highest concentration of registered sex offenders in the St. Louis metro. He bought building after building, held them while he planned the district on a map a friend drew for him, because the moment he rehabbed one — like My Cafe on Sutton — prices on the rest shot up.

What he built from his company: My Cafe, Momo’s, Boogaloo’s, space for Pywacket kids’ theater, and The Focal Point — then donated the theater space and plotted a family district around kids. The portfolio eventually sold for about $3.4 million, but not before a fire sale: while his daughter Mia was fighting cancer at the hospital, he was there instead of on-site, the city turned, deals he counted on did not hold, and he had to let it all go at once.

The St. Louis Business Journal covered the plan on October 26, 2003 under the headline “Pied Piper of Maplewood.” By then he had spent close to $4 million over about six years buying property along Sutton, with several million more planned. The article names the inspiration plainly: his three-year-old daughter Mia, who had come through neurosurgery, radiation, and chemotherapy. He was in talks with a children’s theater organization, a circus company, and a restaurant operator about joining the district, and the second floors of several commercial buildings were to become condominiums — the sales subsidizing rent low enough that children’s organizations could actually afford Sutton Boulevard. He had also bought an apartment building overlooking the Sutton Loop to convert. Much of it was financed with money he had made developing real estate in Florida. Full citation on the Media page.

He still wanted to do for the Sutton Loop what Joe Edwards did for the University City Loop. The Channel 9 piece on this page is the clearest statement of what he was trying to do — and why giving back to kids became the mission.

Florida and Michigan

Development did not stop at the metro. In Cape San Blas and Mexico Beach on the Gulf, he bought beachfront by the acre, found a legal way to subdivide it, and sold smaller parcels — a model that generated serious capital. Much of that rolled into Maplewood through a 1031 tax-free exchange, and he became deeply versed in investment-property strategy.

At various points he held about ten properties — roughly five rentals, about four house rehabs, all the Maplewood district buildings, a $400,000 condo at the Lake of the Ozarks (unit one in the building), a $200,000 home in the Benton Harbor, Michigan area, and a Cape San Blas home until a hurricane took half of it; he sold the remainder. He did not lean on long-term renters — in his experience they tear a place up. The portfolio was more buy, subdivide, rehab, and exchange than landlord play.
