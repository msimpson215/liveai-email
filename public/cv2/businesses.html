STAT testing — the industry he originated

In 1993 it did not exist. Someone could call — often from a 411 / foreign listing — give a zip code, get a risk assessment, go to an existing patient service center for a blood draw, and have results in about two days. He did not invent labs. He did not invent patient service centers. He connected them. Clients, not patients. People who needed a test. He could still crank that system out today. He ran it through 2016.

He had accounts with about seven or eight laboratories — SmithKline Beecham, Unilab, LabCorp, Quest, Roche, National, and others. He sent the doctor’s order. They did the phlebotomy. Results came back to him. By 1996 the panels were full STD work that did not need a physical exam: herpes, syphilis, chlamydia, gonorrhea, hepatitis. Not HPV — that takes an exam.

He was early in offering PCR (polymerase chain reaction) to look for HIV before antibodies showed up. The National AIDS Hotline fought it. They were still telling people you could get HIV from kissing. Now PCR is the test. The health department’s wait was what he called two weeks of hell. That is the gap he closed.

By 2000, people who had worked for him had stood up about five copycat companies. None of those are still in business. You cannot plagiarize the creativity of the original. In 1994 a dispute over counseling without an in-person visit interrupted testing for months. He put his own money into getting it resolved. This page does not invent the caption of the case.

Specialized Testing Services — the first national network

Not his first network. The first — many labs, one system. Annual gross sales about $1.4 million. Quest later tried to run the same play nationally and, in his account, could not pull it off the way he did. That is the business he is proudest of from this chapter.

Medical testing center

Same pattern, later chapter: medical testing companies, including Medical Testing Solutions. Annual gross about $4 million.

Legal DNA paternity was the core — chain of custody for child custody and child support through the courts. Someone called looking for a legal test. Home kits: he usually told them to go to Walgreens. A later DNA shop in Columbia, Illinois, around 2015, did about $40,000 a month. That is a different number from the $4 million above.

STD calls were the same phone: risk assessment first. If they did not need a test, he said so. If they did, he recommended the panel and sent them to a patient service center — LabCorp, Quest, SmithKline, Unilab, Roche, National, whoever had the bricks and mortar.

Restaurants

Maya Cafe on Sutton — he built it out from a shell. It was the anchor for making Maplewood cool again. When the opening plan failed, he took it over, brought in restaurant people, and turned it around. He later walked away from operating it.

Momo’s: he put up about $10,000. It was open two or three months and doing well. Then he had to pull the money. Boogaloo’s: he put up the money and the building. He never opened it. He sold the building. He has never even been inside. That operating history also informs the later fundraising proposal for kids’ hospitals: a restaurant-based giving model, not a speech.
